General contracting reputation works differently from specialty trades. When a homeowner hires a plumber, they're evaluating speed and technical competence. When they hire a GC for a $100,000 remodel, they're evaluating someone they'll work with for months, who will manage their home, coordinate a dozen subcontractors, and handle every complication that arises. Reviews need to communicate a different set of signals.

The most valuable GC reviews don't just say "great work" — they say "delivered on time," "communicated every change," "handled the unexpected foundation issue without drama," "the project came in under the revised budget." Those details are what homeowners evaluating a large project hire on. Your review strategy should be designed to produce that kind of content.

## Ask timing for general contractor projects

**Remodels and large renovations:** Ask at the final walkthrough — when you walk the homeowner through the completed space, confirm the punch list is done, and get verbal sign-off. This is the peak of the project relationship. Make the ask in person, then follow up by text the next morning.

For projects over $100,000 or 3+ months in duration, consider a two-stage approach:
1. Ask at walkthrough as normal
2. Send a follow-up 2 weeks later: "Just checking in — how are you finding the [space] now that you've had a chance to live with it? If we delivered what we promised, a detailed Google review would mean the world to us."

The 2-week review often produces more specific and more valuable content because the homeowner has experienced the results, not just the finished product.

**New construction:** The occupation date is your ask moment — when the homeowner gets their keys and takes possession. This is an extremely high-satisfaction moment. Have your ask ready.

**Commercial GC work:** Reviews for commercial projects often go on Google Maps for your business location (not the project site). The decision-maker who hired you is the right reviewer — they can speak to budget management, communication, and project delivery. Ask them specifically, not the general staff.

## Prompts that produce better GC reviews

Generic GC reviews ("great job, highly recommend") are less effective than reviews that contain project specifics. You can encourage more specific reviews with natural prompts during the ask:

**Instead of:** "Mind leaving us a Google review?"

**Try:** "Mind leaving us a Google review? If you mention what the project was — like the kitchen remodel or the addition — and how the process went for you, that really helps future homeowners evaluate whether we're the right fit for their project."

This prompt doubles as compliance-safe (no rating specification, no incentive) and produces more useful review content. Homeowners respond well to being given a framework — "mention the project and how the process went" is both easy to follow and produces the signal future homeowners are looking for.

## Responding to schedule and communication complaints

Schedule delays and communication issues are the most common GC review negatives. Every contractor has them — large projects are inherently complex. What differentiates top-rated GCs isn't the absence of complaints, it's how they're handled publicly.

**Response framework for schedule complaints:**
1. Acknowledge the delay ("You're right that the project took longer than the original timeline")
2. Explain without excusing ("The [permit delay/subcontractor issue/material backorder] added time we didn't anticipate")
3. Take ownership of the communication ("Where I could have done better was keeping you updated through those delays — that's a fair criticism")
4. State what you've changed ("We've since added [specific improvement] to how we communicate schedule changes")
5. Invite offline resolution ("Please reach out if you have further concerns about the project")

The homeowner reading this exchange six months later isn't evaluating the delay — they're evaluating how you handle being held accountable. A measured, accountable response converts better than a defensive one or no response at all.

## Platform priorities for general contractors

**Google Business Profile — primary**

All residential remodeling research starts on Google. Local pack ranking is critical for GCs doing residential work. Your review profile is the primary trust signal.

**Houzz — high priority for design-forward or premium GCs**

Houzz is where homeowners planning significant remodels browse contractor portfolios and reviews. For GCs doing kitchens, bathrooms, additions, and renovations above $50,000 average project size, Houzz is one of your most valuable review platforms. Build a Houzz profile with project photos and actively cultivate reviews there.

**BBB — due diligence for large commercial projects**

Commercial clients and homeowners evaluating large GC relationships check BBB. Maintain your BBB profile and keep it complaint-free.

**Angi — worth attention for residential leads**

If you run Angi leads or Angi Ads, your Angi rating feeds lead quality. Ask for Angi reviews from Angi-sourced customers.

General contracting review strategy is slower-building than specialty trades because job volume is lower and each project relationship is more complex.
