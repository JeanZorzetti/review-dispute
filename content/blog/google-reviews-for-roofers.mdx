Roofing sits in an unusual position among contractor trades. Projects are large, infrequent, and often triggered by damage or insurance claims — which means the emotional context of the job is more complicated than HVAC maintenance or a plumbing fix. A homeowner dealing with a roof replacement after a hailstorm isn't in the same headspace as someone who just got their AC serviced on a clear summer day. Your review strategy needs to account for that.

This guide covers the full roofing-specific review picture: when to ask, which platforms to prioritize, how to handle insurance-job reviews, what compliance looks like in the trades, and how to protect your profile when competitors or unhappy customers attack.

## When to ask: the roofing-specific timing window

<AnswerBox>
For roofing, the best ask moment is the post-completion walkthrough — when you walk the homeowner around the finished job, show them the work, answer final questions, and get visual sign-off. This is peak satisfaction before the invoice is fully processed, before they're thinking about the next project, and while they're still emotionally invested in the outcome. Ask in person at that moment, then follow up by text the next morning.
</AnswerBox>

Roofing has a timing challenge that other trades don't: the job is on the roof. The homeowner can't touch it or inspect it closely the way they can a new HVAC system or repaired pipe. The walkthrough is your moment to bring them into the quality narrative — show them the flashing work, the ridge cap, the cleanup job, the haul-away. When a homeowner is nodding and saying "looks great," that's your window.

**What the in-person ask sounds like for roofers:**

*"Really glad we could get this done before the next rain. Everything looks solid — new flashing on all the penetrations, ridge cap is tight, and the crew cleaned up as they went. Quick favor: if you wouldn't mind leaving us a Google review, it means a lot for a company our size. I'll text you the link right now while we're both here."*

Then text the link immediately — this is important for roofing specifically. The homeowner is standing in their yard with their phone. The friction of "I'll do it later" collapses dramatically when the link is already on their phone.

**For insurance jobs, adjust the timing:**

Insurance-claim replacements complicate the ask timeline. The homeowner is often stressed during the claim process, and if the supplement hasn't been resolved or they're waiting on a check, they're not in a mindset to leave a positive review. The right timing for insurance jobs:

- **Before final approval:** do not ask for a review — the job isn't done from the customer's perspective
- **After claim is settled and job is complete:** ask at walkthrough as normal
- **If there were delays or frustrations during the claim process:** acknowledge those in your ask ("I know the supplement process took longer than any of us wanted — appreciate your patience through all of that. If we took good care of you on the actual installation, a Google review would mean the world to us.")

## Platform priorities for roofers

Not all review platforms are equal for roofing. Here's where to focus and why:

**Google Business Profile — primary platform, highest leverage**

Every lead generation dollar you spend on Google (Ads, local SEO, map pack) depends on your GBP review profile. Quantity, recency, and response behavior all feed the local ranking algorithm. For roofers, GBP reviews are the primary reputation asset — nothing else comes close.

Target: 4.5+ stars, 50+ reviews minimum to be competitive in suburban markets, 100+ to dominate in competitive metros.

**Angi (formerly Angie's List) — important if you buy Angi leads**

If you run Angi Leads (formerly HomeAdvisor) or Angi Ads, your Angi rating directly affects the leads you receive and their quality. Angi actively shows ratings to homeowners evaluating leads, so a low Angi rating costs you even when a homeowner found you through a non-Angi search. Ask every Angi-sourced customer for a review on Angi specifically.

**Houzz — relevant for premium residential roofing**

Houzz is where homeowners doing significant home improvement research browse contractor portfolios. For roofers doing higher-end work (slate, metal, architectural shingles), a Houzz presence with photos and reviews signals quality to design-conscious homeowners. If your average job is under $10,000, Houzz is a low priority.

**BBB — due-diligence credibility, not a review driver**

Very few homeowners proactively seek BBB reviews. They check BBB when they're doing due diligence on a large contractor commitment — a $20,000+ roof replacement. Maintain your BBB profile and respond to any complaints, but don't invest in active review collection there.

**Yelp — skip for most roofing markets**

Yelp reviews in the home services/contracting space are increasingly irrelevant for most markets, and Yelp's algorithm actively penalizes solicited reviews. Unless you're in a market with high Yelp penetration (certain metro areas on the West Coast), don't invest here.

## Compliance considerations specific to roofing

<AnswerBox>
Roofing has one compliance consideration other trades don't: insurance adjuster relationships. Some contractors are tempted to ask insurance adjusters or public adjusters to leave reviews. This is a fake review situation — adjusters are not customers and cannot post reviews of your business as if they were homeowners. Everything else in review compliance (no gating, no incentives, no buying) applies the same as any other trade.
</AnswerBox>

The standard rules apply to roofers:
- Ask every completed-job homeowner — not just the happy ones
- No incentives (no "leave us a review and we'll waive the deductible supplement fee")
- No gating (don't filter by satisfaction score before sending the review link)
- No fake reviews from adjusters, suppliers, or employees

**One roofing-specific watch item:** some roofing companies ask their materials suppliers, subcontractors, or insurance contacts to leave positive reviews to pad their count. These are fake reviews from non-customers — they violate Google's policy and, if detected, can trigger a "suspicious activity" notice. Don't do it.

## Protecting your profile during storm season

High-volume storm seasons are when roofing review profiles get attacked. Competitors game the lead ecosystem, and a surge of negative reviews — often from fake accounts — can precede a direct mail campaign targeting your service area. Signs of a coordinated attack:

- Multiple one-star reviews appear within 48 hours
- Reviewers have no review history or recently created accounts
- Review content is generic ("bad company," "terrible experience") with no job-specific details
- You have no record of the reviewer as a customer

When this happens, respond calmly to each review (for the benefit of readers, not the fake reviewer), then report each one for policy violations. Document the accounts involved. ## The roofing review ask system in practice

**Setup (one afternoon before busy season):**
- [ ] Generate your direct Google review link
- [ ] Create a short URL and QR code
- [ ] Add QR code to your job-completion invoice/leave-behind card
- [ ] Load the review link into your field-service software's job-completion automation
- [ ] Write the in-person ask script; train every project manager and salesperson

**Per-job routine:**
- [ ] At walkthrough: make the in-person ask and text the link on the spot
- [ ] Day 1 after completion: automated text fires (if not already reviewed)
- [ ] Day 5: second follow-up fires (if no review)
- [ ] Day 7: mark job as complete in review tracking

**Monthly review:**
- [ ] Count new reviews vs. completed jobs (target: 12–18% conversion)
- [ ] Respond to every new review (aim for within 48 hours)
- [ ] Check for any suspicious review activity
- [ ] Compare your count and average against your top 3 local competitors
