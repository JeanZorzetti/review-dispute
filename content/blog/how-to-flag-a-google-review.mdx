Flagging a Google review sends it to Google's moderation queue for a policy check; it does not delete the review by itself. This guide walks through the exact steps on desktop and the Maps mobile app, how to pick the right violation category, how to track the status, and what to do if Google denies the report.

## What Flagging a Google Review Actually Does

When you click Report review on a Google review, you're not deleting anything yourself — you're routing that specific review into Google's moderation queue, where it gets checked against the Maps user-generated content policy. The **flag** itself carries no legal or contractual weight; it's a request, and Google's automated systems or a human moderator decide whether the content actually violates a rule.

This is different from disputing a review through a lawyer's letter or a complaint to a licensing board — flagging is the fast, free, built-in first step, and in the large majority of legitimate cases it's also the only step you need. Skipping it and going straight to an escalation email rarely works, because Google's support team usually points you back to the standard **report** flow.

Anyone with a signed-in **Google Account** can flag a review, not just the business owner. A customer, an employee, or an unrelated bystander who spots an obviously fake review can report it — though only the owner or manager sees the case move through the Business Profile's reviews tool with a status attached.

### Why Google gates removal behind a report

Google's own numbers show why the queue exists: its 2025 Trust & Safety Report for Maps disclosed that roughly **292 million** policy-violating reviews were blocked or removed that year, and about 22% of all review submissions were classified as violations. A blanket auto-delete system at that volume would just as easily wipe out real, if harsh, feedback — so every flag still gets checked against a specific rule.

Flagging correctly matters more than flagging fast. A report filed under the wrong category, with no supporting detail, is one of the most common reasons a clearly fake review stays up — the fix is picking the right violation type and knowing what **evidence** Google actually looks for.

## How to Flag a Google Review on Desktop

On a computer, there are two nearly identical ways to reach the same report form: through Google Search results for your business, or through Google Maps. Both use the same underlying **flagging system**, so pick whichever is already open.

### Flagging from Google Search

- Search for your business name on Google
- Open the Business Profile panel on the right side of the results
- Click the Reviews tab and find the review
- Click the three-dot menu next to that review
- Select Report review, choose a reason, and submit

### Flagging from Google Maps on a computer

- Open Google Maps and search for the business
- Click Reviews below the business name
- Scroll to the specific review
- Click the three vertical dots (kebab menu) on that review
- Select Report review and choose the violation category

Both paths lead to the same short list of report reasons, though the exact panel — an inline menu or a pop-up window — can vary depending on whether you're on Search or Maps. If you manage the profile, the same review also shows up in the Business Profile's **Manage reviews** section, where you can track it after reporting.

## How to Flag a Review From the Google Maps Mobile App

On a phone, flagging happens inside the **Google Maps app** itself rather than through a browser, and the tap sequence is nearly identical on iOS and Android.

### The tap-by-tap steps

- Open the Google Maps app and search for the business
- Tap the business name, then tap Reviews
- Scroll to the review you want to report
- Tap the three-dot menu on that review
- Tap Report review and select the reason that fits

A short **confirmation banner** appears once the report goes through, usually along the lines of a note that Google will review it against policy. There's no in-app tracking number, so screenshot the review and the confirmation before it disappears from view.

If the problem is the **reviewer's profile** rather than one review — a pattern of fake posts across multiple businesses, for example — tap the reviewer's name to open their profile, then use the More menu to report the account itself, separate from reporting the individual review.

Reporting on mobile does not move any faster through Google's queue than reporting on desktop; the app is just a more convenient entry point into the same **moderation system**.

## Flagging as a Customer vs. Reporting as the Business Owner

The public **Report review** button and the Business Profile's built-in **Manage reviews** tool both submit to the same review queue, but they aren't identical experiences, and knowing the difference changes how you should plan a dispute.

| Aspect | Flagging via Maps or Search (anyone) | Reporting via Business Profile Manage reviews (owner) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who can do it | Any signed-in Google Account | Verified owner or manager only |
| Status visibility | No tracking after submission | Pending, needs info, or resolved status shown |
| Evidence attachment | Reason only, no file upload | Reason plus space to describe supporting evidence |
| Best for | A single obviously fake review spotted by anyone | Building a documented case with invoices or records |
| Appeal option | Not available to non-owners | One-time appeal if the report is denied |

For a business owner, the Manage reviews path is almost always the better first move, because it's the only route that shows a **status** — pending, needs more information, or resolved — instead of leaving you guessing whether anything happened.

When the profile is unclaimed, **suspended**, or access has been lost, the owner-side tool isn't available, and the public flag through Maps or Search becomes the only option until access is restored, which is its own separate fix.

### When someone else flags on your behalf

A customer, employee, or even an uninvolved third party can flag a fake review before you ever see it, which sometimes gets a case moving faster — but Google still credits the owner's **documented evidence** more than an anonymous report with no context attached.

## Choosing the Right Violation Category

Every report form asks for a reason, and picking the wrong one is one of the fastest ways to get a legitimate case denied — the **category** has to match what the review actually does wrong, not just how unfair it feels.

- Spam — ads, phone numbers, links, or repeated identical text
- Off topic — not about an actual experience with the business
- Conflict of interest — from a competitor, employee, or the owner
- Fake engagement — the reviewer was never a real customer
- Harassment, hate speech, or profanity — personal attacks or slurs
- Personal information — doxxing or private details about a person

Choosing off topic for a review that's actually a **conflict of interest** case, say from a competitor, forces a moderator to evaluate the wrong claim, and a rejected report can make a second attempt on the same review look repetitive rather than persuasive.

### Matching what you saw to a category

If the reviewer never appears in your job records, use fake engagement; if the review threatens a bad rating unless you pay or issue a refund, that's **extortion** territory, which Google now treats as its own high-priority case rather than plain harassment.

Google's April 2026 policy update also tightened the definition of manipulated engagement: directing staff to hit a review quota, or reviews that name specific employees as part of an internal incentive program, now falls under the same **fake engagement** rules used to catch review farms — worth knowing before you build, or flag, an in-house review-request habit.

## Tracking Status, Appeals, and What Happens Next

Once a report is filed, the business owner can check its progress in the Business Profile app or dashboard under **Reviews**, without needing a separate case number.

- Pending — Google has the report and hasn't finished reviewing it
- Needs more information — an extra evidence request was sent
- Resolved — the review was removed, or Google found no violation

Google's own guidance points to a review window measured in days rather than hours — commonly a **few business days**, sometimes closer to two weeks for a borderline case — and re-flagging the same review repeatedly does not speed that up.

### If Google says the review doesn't violate policy

A denial isn't necessarily final: Google allows a **one-time appeal** on a rejected report, and that appeal is the place to add the specific detail the first report lacked — an invoice number, a screenshot, a timestamp — rather than just restating that the review is unfair.

Google has also been investing in faster, AI-assisted detection — its Maps team says **Gemini models** now help flag manipulated edits and extortion attempts before a human opens the case — but that automation supplements report review; it doesn't replace picking the right category and attaching real evidence.
