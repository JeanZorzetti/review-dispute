Every contractor eventually stares at a one-star review and asks the same question: is this even real? The stakes aren't academic. Report a real review as fake and Google denies it, your credibility with their triage drops, and the second attempt gets harder. Sit on a genuinely fake review because you weren't sure, and it quietly costs you calls for years. The line between the two is more learnable than most business owners think — it just isn't the line most people look for. Tone doesn't tell you. Anger doesn't tell you. Records do.

## How can you tell if a Google review is fake?

<AnswerBox>
A Google review is fake when it describes an experience that never happened. The most reliable way to tell is to search your own records — invoices, job tickets, call logs, service addresses — for the reviewer's name and the job they describe. If nothing matches, and the review also carries two or three supporting signals (a brand-new profile, no job-specific detail, suspicious timing), you're looking at a fake review that Google will remove once you show them the pattern. Tone, anger, and unfairness are not evidence of anything.
</AnswerBox>

The mistake almost everyone makes is starting from the review's *emotion* and working backwards. A review that calls you a scam artist feels fake because no real customer would say that — except real customers say that all the time when they're furious. Meanwhile, the smoothest, most reasonable-sounding review in your profile might have been written by a competitor's marketing intern in another state.

Start from the record instead. The nine signs below are ordered by how much weight they actually carry when you file a report.

## The 9 red flags of a fake Google review

### 1. The reviewer isn't in your customer records

This is the one that matters. Search your invoicing software, your CRM, your job scheduler, your phone logs, and your email for the reviewer's name, then for any variation of it — first name only, last name only, a spouse's name at the same address. Search the neighborhood or job type they mention. If there is no invoice, no estimate, no ticket, and no call, you have the only fact Google can act on: this person is not describing an experience with your business, because there was no experience.

Everything else in this list exists to reinforce this one.

<Callout type="tip">
Do this search **before** you look at the reviewer's profile. If the name does turn up in your records, you're dealing with an unhappy customer, not a fake — and the whole playbook changes from removal to [response and recovery](/blog/how-to-respond-to-negative-google-reviews).
</Callout>

### 2. The review describes services you don't offer

Fakes are written by people who never stood in your driveway. So they hedge with generic trade language, and the hedge slips. A roofer gets a review complaining about a botched water heater. An HVAC company gets accused of overcharging for a "duct cleaning special" they've never run. A residential-only electrician gets blasted about a commercial job.

This one is powerful because it's objectively checkable. You can prove you don't offer the service. When you report, say exactly that: the review describes work this business does not perform.

### 3. The profile is a ghost account

Click the reviewer's name and read the profile like an investigator:

- **Total reviews: 1.** The account exists to post this and nothing else.
- **No profile photo**, or a stock-looking headshot.
- **Generic name** — a first name and a single initial, or an obviously assembled name.
- **Account created days before the review**, with no photos, no other contributions.

None of these prove anything alone. Plenty of real, furious customers create an account just to leave you a one-star. But a ghost account **plus** no record of the person in your books is a pattern, and patterns are what Google's reviewers respond to.

### 4. The timing is too convenient

Fake reviews cluster because the person writing them is on a schedule, not a customer journey. Watch for:

- Two or three one-stars inside 48 hours, from unrelated profiles, after months of nothing.
- A one-star that lands days after you won a big contract, fired someone, or ended a partnership.
- A negative review that arrives right when a competitor launches a promotion in your service area.
- A wave of one-stars right after you reported *their* fake reviews.

Real dissatisfaction arrives one at a time, at random. Coordinated dissatisfaction arrives in a batch.

### 5. There's no job-specific detail — anywhere

Real customers, even brief ones, leak specifics. The day it rained. The technician's first name. The price. What broke. How long they waited. What the invoice said.

Fake reviews stay at 30,000 feet: "Terrible service. Unprofessional. Overcharged. Avoid this company." Four sentences of pure adjective, zero nouns from your actual business. It's the writing of someone who has to describe a job they never saw.

<Callout type="warning">
Careful with this one. Absence of detail is suggestive, not conclusive — some real customers are just terse. Never build a report on wording alone. Wording supports the records; it never replaces them.
</Callout>

### 6. The reviewer's history looks like a review farm

Open the profile and scan every review they've ever left. The tell isn't the count — it's the *incoherence*:

- Reviews for businesses in cities hundreds of miles apart, posted in the same week.
- Twelve reviews in three days, then silence for a year.
- Five-star raves for a handful of businesses and one-star attacks on their direct competitors.
- Reviews in a category no human uses this way — four plumbers reviewed in one afternoon.

That last pattern is often the fingerprint of a purchased review campaign, and it's exactly what Google's spam policy exists to catch.

### 7. It's obviously about a different business

Sometimes the review is real — just not about you. It names a company that isn't yours, describes a storefront you don't have, or complains about a location on the other side of the country. Google's own policy treats content that isn't about your business as off-topic and removable, and this is one of the easiest reports to win because there's nothing to argue about.

### 8. It comes with a demand

If the review — or a message that arrived alongside it — offers to take the rating down in exchange for money, a refund, free work, or a discount, you're not looking at a review at all. You're looking at [review extortion](/blog/remove-extortion-review-threats), and Google treats it as a policy violation independent of whether the person was ever a customer.

Do not pay. Screenshot the demand. Report it as extortion, not as a fake review.

### 9. The reviewer has a conflict of interest

Google prohibits reviews from people with a stake in your business doing badly: current or former employees, competitors, ex-partners, a family member of a competitor. These reviews are often the most convincing ones you'll ever read, because the author actually knows your operation — which is exactly why the policy exists.

If you can show the connection, this is a strong report. Screenshot whatever proves it: an employment record, a LinkedIn profile showing they work for a competitor, an old email thread.

## 4 signs that look fake but almost never are

This is the half of the guide most articles skip, and it's the half that saves you from a denial.

### A furious, insulting tone

"Scam." "Crooks." "Worst company in the state." Vicious language is not a policy violation. Google protects opinions, including violently expressed ones. If the person is in your records, that review is staying up — and your energy belongs in the reply, not in the report button.

### A one-star with no text at all

Silent one-stars are legal on Google and infuriating for exactly that reason: there's nothing to disprove. They aren't removable on their own. They only become reportable if you can establish who left it and show they were never a customer or have a conflict of interest.

### A review from someone who isn't the name on the invoice

The homeowner's spouse, the adult child who let you in, the tenant, the property manager, the roommate who was home during the install — all of them experienced your service, so all of them can review you. The name not matching your invoice is not evidence of a fake. Search the *address* and the job before you conclude anything.

### Details that are wrong

A real customer can misremember the price, blame the wrong technician, exaggerate the wait, or genuinely misunderstand what you did and why. Being wrong isn't the same as lying, and Google will not adjudicate whose memory is correct. Correct the record publicly in your reply — that's what future customers actually read.

<Definition term="Fake vs. removable">
A review can be fake and still stay up if you can't demonstrate it, and a review can be entirely real and still come down if it breaks a different rule (off-topic, spam, conflict of interest, prohibited content). "Is it fake?" is a question about the truth. "Is it removable?" is a question about evidence and policy. You need the second one answered before you report.
</Definition>

## Before you report: build the two-minute evidence file

A report with evidence attached and a specific policy named gets a very different result than a report that says "this is fake." Spend two minutes on this and your odds improve materially:

1. **Screenshot the review** — full text, star rating, reviewer name, and date visible.
2. **Screenshot the reviewer's profile** — review count, account age, other reviews. These get scrubbed the moment a farm account is cleaned up, and your evidence disappears with them.
3. **Pull your records** — the search you ran that returned nothing, or the invoice showing the job never existed. A screenshot of an empty customer search for the reviewer's name is a real exhibit.
4. **Note the timing** — what happened in your business in the week before the review landed.
5. **Name the policy** — fake engagement, conflict of interest, off-topic, prohibited content, or spam. Reporting "this is unfair" gets ignored; reporting "this violates the fake engagement policy because the reviewer has no record of service and the account exists solely to post this review" gets read.

## The rule that keeps you out of trouble

Report the review you can *document*, not the review you *resent*. Contractors who win removals aren't the ones with the strongest opinions about which reviews are unfair — they're the ones who searched their records first, screenshotted the profile before it vanished, named a specific policy, and let the honest one-stars go so their reports carried weight when it counted.
